01 / 14 Introduction

Scan waste.

WasteWise is a Final Year Project on automated waste understanding: a two-stage deep-learning pipeline (YOLO26m finds every object, a ConvNeXt classifier verifies the material), backed by a classical machine-learning branch for explainable evidence. The problem, the goal, the system

Manual waste sorting is slow and error-prone, and recycling streams are easily contaminated. WasteWise localizes each waste object in a photo, verifies its material, and routes it to the correct bin, with an honest, audited account of what works and what does not.

Objective 1 Detect & classify waste in real photos

Localize every object with YOLO26m, then verify the material of each crop across 7 classes with a dedicated classifier.

Objective 2 Evaluate honestly

Audit the evaluation sets with perceptual hashing, quarantine leaked images, and report only clean-test numbers.

Objective 3 Explainable evidence

Keep a classical ML branch on 637 handcrafted features beside the deep pipeline, so every decision has an interpretable baseline.

Everything in the next thirteen chapters is a zoom-in on one box of this diagram.

Look at the two model boxes in the middle. Those are the only two trained components — everything else is plumbing. And the design decision worth defending is that they are two models, not one. A single classifier on a whole photo has to answer “what material is this picture?”, which is the wrong question when a photo contains four objects of three materials. Localizing first turns one hard question into several easy ones.

Why not a single end-to-end detector with material classes? It was tried: that is the YOLO26m detector's own 7-class head. The confusion matrix in the Deep Learning chapter shows its errors are almost entirely background, not material, so a second stage that only judges material on an already-cropped object is the cheaper fix.

And this is the literal screen that architecture turns into. You will see it running in the Live Demo.

Notice the per-class score list on the right. It is there on purpose: a single top-1 label hides whether the model was confident or barely picked a winner. The confidence meter shows the fused score, not the classifier's raw softmax — detector confidence and classifier confidence are combined before anything is shown, so the number on screen is the number the routing decision actually used.

Why show the Background class to a user at all? Because it is the veto. When Background wins, the system says it found no waste there rather than guessing a material, and users need to see that distinction.

02 / 14 Dataset Overview

Six public sources, each on its own terms first.

No single public dataset covers waste detection well. This chapter takes them one at a time — what each source contains, how its classes are distributed, and what it can and cannot contribute — and only then shows the merge. Read in that order the merge stops being an assumption and becomes a conclusion: TACO has real litter photos but sparse labels, TrashNet has clean studio crops but no boxes, WaDaBa resolves plastic subtypes, GINI frames garbage-vs-clean scenes, and the Roboflow projects plus PlastOPol supply extra field imagery. Merged, the generation these charts are drawn from holds 31,379 images and 67,385 boxes (24,668 / 3,298 / 3,413 train/valid/test) — one of three successive rebuilds, tabulated at the end of this chapter.

i

Two acquisition regimes, six sources. TrashNet and WaDaBa contribute studio-style imagery; TACO, GINI, the Roboflow projects and PlastOPol contribute real-world scenes. The written report names the merge inputs at the generation that feeds the deployed models: TACO for field imagery, Kaggle Garbage Classification (Abla, 2021) as the curated studio comparison domain, PlastOPol’s 2,418 citizen-science litter photos as the later field-domain expansion, and multiple Roboflow community re-exports of all of the above — which is precisely what caused the leakage problem in the next chapter. The hard-case classifier crops add RealWaste. Each source below is described independently, because pooling them first is what hides the studio/field divide that the rest of the site keeps running into.

A · Each source on its own

One dataset per scene: what it holds, and what it cannot give.

First source: TACO — the one that most resembles the deployment condition.

Notice the plastic bar dwarfing the rest. That imbalance is real-world litter composition, not a sampling mistake. TACO is the most valuable and the most awkward source. Valuable because the photos are genuine litter in genuine scenes; awkward because objects are small, occluded, and long-tailed. Almost every hard number later in this walkthrough — especially field recall — is limited by TACO-like images.

Was the long tail rebalanced? Yes — by capping per-class counts after the merge (the Preprocessing chapter), which trims the plastic surplus rather than inventing minority-class data.

Second source: TrashNet — the exact opposite of TACO in every way that matters.

Notice how even the bars are. Even coverage is convenient, and that convenience is also the trap. TrashNet's clean single-object photos are what make the classifier's benchmark numbers look good. They are also why a model trained mostly on this source collapses on real photos — the Machine Learning chapter's domain-transfer heatmap puts a number on exactly that collapse.

Why keep it at all? Because it supplies the class balance and the volume the field sources cannot. The fix is not to drop it — it is to make sure the evaluation set is not dominated by it.

Third source: GINI, which contributes something none of the others do.

Just two bars — GINI is a binary dataset, with no material labels at all. It was kept for its negatives as much as its positives. Clean-scene images are what teach the Background class what “no waste here” looks like, and the Background veto is the single biggest source of false-alarm suppression in the deployed pipeline.

How does a binary dataset map into a 7-class vocabulary? Positives fold into the catch-all class, negatives into Background. It contributes scene context, not material identity.

Fourth source: WaDaBa — where a deliberate piece of information was thrown away.

Look at the polymer subtype bars: WaDaBa labels PET, HDPE, PP and so on separately. In this project they all collapse into one class. That is finer than the target vocabulary, so it was flattened to Plastic. Discarding label detail feels wasteful, but a class the other five sources cannot supply examples for would be trained almost entirely on one dataset's studio look — the domain-confound problem in Fig 2.7.

Are polymer subtypes future work? Yes, and a natural extension — but it needs field photos of labelled polymers, which is a data problem rather than a modelling one.

Put a face on the abstraction. This is what “studio domain” means.

The backgrounds: all identical. The lighting: all identical. A model can reach very high accuracy on images like these by learning the background and the lighting rather than the material. That is not a hypothetical — the Machine Learning chapter shows a classifier trained on studio images scoring 39.0% on field images of the same seven materials. Keep this grid in mind when that number appears.

How do we know the deployed model does not do this? Two pieces of evidence. Grad-CAM in the Experimental Results chapter shows activation on the object surface rather than the background, and the domain confusion matrices in the Deep Learning chapter show the deployed classifier holding 91.3% on field crops.

B · Only now, the merge

Six independent sources, one YOLO-format dataset with a shared 7-class vocabulary.

Before the merge can be defended, one thing has to be true: no single dataset made it unnecessary.

Look at the gaps, not the bars. Every column has at least one material it does not cover, and no column covers both studio and field conditions. Merging public datasets is normally a shortcut that gets criticised, so it needs a reason. The reason is here: the target is 7 materials photographed in the wild, and no released dataset provides that. The cost of merging is label-vocabulary conflict and duplicate images: the vocabulary is unified by the class mapping in the Preprocessing chapter, and the duplicates are what the leakage audit in the EDA chapter goes looking for.

Why not just collect original data? Because 31,379 images with box-level labels is far beyond what a single-semester FYP can annotate. The honest trade is more data of mixed provenance, audited hard, over less data of clean provenance.

The most uncomfortable figure in this chapter — and the reason the six sources above were introduced one at a time.

Pick any class whose bar is dominated by a single source colour. Where a class comes overwhelmingly from one dataset, the model can score well on that class by recognising the dataset rather than the material. Class identity and acquisition domain are partly entangled, and no amount of class balancing fixes it, because the confound is in the pixels, not the counts.

What was done about it? Two things. It motivated a domain-split evaluation rather than a single pooled number — producing the domain-transfer heatmap in the Machine Learning chapter — and it is why the deployed classifier was fine-tuned on the detector's own crops, so training and production share a distribution.

03 / 14 Exploratory Data Analysis

What the merged data actually looks like, including its bugs.

EDA here asks three questions about the merged dataset itself: is the class balance usable, how small are the objects, and can the test set be trusted? The third found a real evaluation bug: perceptual hashing showed 2,406 test images (43%) of an earlier classifier evaluation set also appeared in training, inflating its accuracy from an honest 91.77% to 94.30%. All headline numbers on this site are measured after that leakage was quarantined.

i

Scope note. Per-source share-of-class percentages belong to the previous chapter, where each dataset is introduced on its own terms. Every figure below is a property of the merged dataset and its splits, so nothing here mixes datasets into a single percentage.

!

The number that almost shipped. 94.30% looked like a finished result. It wasn’t: 43% of that test set was quietly sitting in training too, recompressed or resized but still the same photo. An honest number that survives an audit (91.77%) beats a flattering one that doesn’t, so every metric on this site is measured after the leaked images were found and removed.

First EDA question: is the class balance usable inside every split, not just overall?

Look at the ratio between the three bars within each class, not the absolute heights. The check is that balance holds within every split, not just overall. A dataset can be globally balanced while its test split is 40% one class — and that makes accuracy a misleading headline. Here the proportions hold, so accuracy and macro-F1 stay close throughout, and that agreement is itself the evidence. The mechanism that produced this balance — capping the majority classes and splitting by dominant class — is the next chapter’s subject; this figure is the check that it worked.

Which metric wins if they ever diverge? Macro-F1, because it weights each class equally regardless of support.

Second question: how small are these objects, really?

Notice the spike at the left edge and the long thin tail to the right. That distribution is why the project reports small-box recall as a separate metric instead of hiding it inside overall recall. A detector can post a respectable mAP while missing most of the mass in that left-hand spike, because the few large objects are easy and numerous enough to carry the average.

What counts as small? Anything below the standard small-object area threshold used by the evaluation scripts — applied identically to every model compared here, so the numbers stay comparable.

Same story as the last figure — but now you can see it is not evenly shared.

Look at the classes pushed past the 50% mark. Those are the litter-in-context classes, and this is where a per-class metric earns its keep. An aggregate small-box number would average the studio-sourced classes, which are easy, against the field-sourced ones, which are not — hiding the fact that difficulty tracks the source dataset rather than the material.

How did this change the project? It made field small-object recall the headline experiment metric in the Experimental Results chapter, ahead of overall mAP.

Third question — and the one that changed the project: can the test set be trusted?

Look at the quarantine step. Nothing is deleted; leaked images are set aside, so the decision stays reversible and inspectable. Exact-duplicate checks had already run during preprocessing and found nothing wrong. Perceptual hashing catches what byte comparison cannot: the same photo re-encoded, resized, or lightly recompressed by a different dataset's export pipeline. On a six-source merge that is not an edge case — it is the norm.

Why not just re-split from scratch? Quarantining preserves the ability to report both numbers side by side. Showing 94.30% and 91.77% on the same model is stronger evidence of a clean process than quietly publishing one number.

This is the leak caught in the act — the most persuasive image in the whole project.

Pick a pair and confirm with your own eyes: it is the same photograph. One copy sat in train, the other in test. 2,406 images — 43% of that evaluation set — were in this state. The model was being tested on photographs it had already been trained on, so it was being rewarded for memorisation. Nothing about the model was broken; the measurement was.

Was the hash threshold tuned to produce a dramatic number? No. It was set first and spot-checked visually afterwards — which is exactly what this figure shows, and the next figure sweeps the threshold to prove the headline does not depend on it. The direction of the error also matters, and it is the honest one: the audit only ever lowered the reported accuracy.

A four-bit threshold is a judgement call, and the whole 43% headline rests on it. So here is the sensitivity check.

Follow the top line from left to right and note how flat it is. At distance 0 the audit is only catching images that are perceptually identical to a training image — the exact re-encoding artefact a Roboflow re-export produces — and that alone accounts for 39.1% of the split. Loosening all the way to four bits adds under four points. Any choice in that range yields the same finding, so the headline is a property of the data rather than of the threshold.

Is that true of every split? No, and the difference is worth stating. The lower lines climb more steeply across the same range — the detector’s test split moves 22.7% to 32.9% — so their contamination is genuinely near-duplicate rather than identical, and those figures should be read as threshold-dependent in a way the headline split’s is not.

04 / 14 Image Preprocessing

Preprocessing, in two domains, on real pixels.

Preprocessing comes after the EDA on purpose: every rule below exists because the EDA found something. Dataset-level cleaning removes invalid label rows and exact duplicates, drops degenerate boxes, re-splits train/valid/test by dominant class, and caps per-class counts. Per-image preprocessing then runs in two domains. In the spatial domain each detected box is cropped with 10 px padding, rejected below 24×24 px, resized to 224×224 for the network and 64×64 for the handcrafted branch, then reduced to grayscale, gradient and mask views. In the frequency domain the same 64×64 crop is mean-centred and transformed with a 2D FFT, and its power is summed over eight radial bands. Every panel below is a real crop through the real code, not a diagram of it.

Six archives in, one dataset out. Every arrow here is a step that had to be defended.

Start with the dominant-class re-split box — the least obvious step, and the one that matters most. Images are split by their dominant class rather than randomly, so a multi-object photo cannot land in train and have its rare class evaluated in test. Random splitting on a detection dataset silently correlates the splits. This is the same family of mistake as the leakage found in the EDA chapter — caught here by design rather than by audit.

What does “validity filtering” remove? Label rows outside image bounds, zero-area and degenerate boxes, and exact-duplicate files. Near-duplicates survive this stage — which is precisely why the perceptual-hash audit was still needed afterwards.

This is what the balancing pass actually bought.

Notice the flat top across the six material bars — roughly 7,100 to 9,000 boxes each — next to the shorter catch-all bar. Balancing here works by capping the majority classes, not by oversampling the minorities. Duplicating minority images would inflate every metric that follows without adding a single new pixel of information. Capping throws data away, which feels wrong — and is the honest option.

Why is the catch-all class deliberately left small? It is not a material anyone routes to a bin. It exists to absorb objects that are clearly waste but not one of the six, so the detector is not forced to mislabel them.

A · Spatial domain

Operations on the pixel grid itself: crop, gate, resize, grayscale, gradient, mask.

Start with the first column and note the aspect ratios: 33×352, 88×80, 154×163. Nothing here is square.

That is the first honest thing this figure shows. Every downstream step forces the crop to a square grid, so a long thin crop is stretched. The alternative — letterboxing to preserve aspect — pads real texture with grey, and for a material classifier texture is the entire signal. Distortion was chosen over padding deliberately, and the augmentation in the next figure is partly there to make the model tolerant of it.

Why two different resize targets? The network wants 224×224 because that is what its pretrained weights expect. The handcrafted branch wants 64×64 because its 637 features were defined at that size, and changing it would shift every feature distribution the deployed classifier was trained on.

Balancing by capping majority classes was the dataset-level answer. This is the image-level one.

Look at the rotation panel and its replicated border: the frame is filled by repeating the edge pixel rather than with black, because a black wedge is a strong artificial edge and the HOG and gradient features would read it as real structure. Every operation here is chosen to be photometrically or geometrically plausible for a waste crop. What is deliberately absent is anything that changes hue — colour is 256 of the 637 features and a large share of the classifier’s evidence, so shifting it would be training the model on a lie.

Is this the same as oversampling? No, and the distinction matters for the leakage story. Duplicating a minority image inflates metrics without adding information. These transforms produce a crop the model has genuinely not seen, and the two right-hand panels are what the loader actually emits.

This is the figure the box-area histogram in the EDA was pointing at all along.

Look at the 23×26 crop and try to name the material. That is the honest test, and it is why the gate exists: at that size there is no texture left, only a colour blob, and the classifier will still return a confident label for it. Refusing to answer is better than a confident guess the interface presents as a result. Note the crop right next to it at 24×24 that does clear the gate — the threshold is a hard line through a continuum, which is exactly what makes it worth stating out loud.

What does the gate cost? Real detections, and the cost is visible rather than absorbed: small-object detection recall is tracked as its own metric throughout the site, so dropping these boxes never quietly improves a headline number.

B · Frequency domain

The same crop read as spatial frequencies: 2D FFT, radial bands, band reconstructions.

Columns 3 and 4 are the point of this figure: each is a real inverse transform of one band only.

Compare them. The low-pass reconstruction keeps the object’s shape and lighting and loses all its surface. The high-pass keeps the surface — the corrugation, the print, the specular speckle — and loses the object. Material identity lives in the second one. That is the whole reason a frequency representation is computed at all rather than trusting the pixel grid: the eight radial band energies measure how much of the crop’s power sits in fine detail versus coarse structure, in nine numbers instead of 4,096 pixels.

Why mean-centre before transforming? Because the DC term is just average brightness. Left in, it dominates the power spectrum and every band ratio becomes a measure of exposure rather than of texture.

Compare the seven panels and the honest verdict is: they look the same. That is worth showing rather than hiding.

The raw averaged spectrum is not a class signature. Every panel is swamped by the DC spike at the centre and by the horizontal and vertical cross, which is not object structure at all — it is the discontinuity at the crop border, an artefact of transforming a non-periodic square patch. Whatever class information exists is buried under both.

So why compute a frequency representation at all? Because the features are not the spectrum — they are eight ratios of energy in radial rings, normalized by total power. Normalizing divides out the DC dominance and the overall exposure that make these panels look alike, and the class ordering that survives is the one measured in the next chapter’s per-class frequency figure. This panel is the control that shows the ratios are doing real work, not the raw transform.

C · What the detector finally trained on

The output of everything above, as Ultralytics sees it.

This is the exact dataset the deployed detector saw, straight out of Ultralytics.

Look at the box width–height scatter in the lower right. The dense cluster near the origin is the whole small-object problem in one plot. Most boxes occupy a small fraction of the frame, and they are concentrated near the centre — photographers aim at what they are photographing. Both facts predict the failure mode measured later: recall, not precision, is what limits this detector.

Why not simply train at higher resolution? It was tried. A 960 px run left validation unchanged and lifted unseen-batch recall by 3.9 points, which did not justify the cost. The Discussion chapter covers what did work instead.

05 / 14 Feature Extraction

637 numbers per crop, and what every one of them is.

Both branches of the system consume the same handcrafted feature vector, so it gets its own chapter rather than a footnote. The deployed extractor (scripts/custom_feature_extractor.py, imported directly by the served API) turns each crop into 637 floats: 256 colour, 47 texture, 10 shape and 324 edge features. Most of them literally are histograms — histogram bins used as features — so this chapter shows them as histograms rather than describing them.

Analysis vector feature_ml_analysis.py

A second 637-D vector, used only for the domain study.

The classical-branch analysis uses a different decomposition of the same budget: 8 spatial statistics (intensity mean, std, percentiles, gradient mean and std, edge density), 9 frequency features (8 radial FFT bands + a high-frequency energy summary), 44 colour and 576 HOG. It exists because those 17 spatial and frequency scalars have physical meaning a histogram bin does not, which is what makes the domain-importance and per-class distribution figures below readable. Both vectors are 637-D; they are not the same 637 numbers, and the figures below say which is which.

i

Why handcrafted features at all, next to a deep network? Three reasons, all measurable. They are the interpretable branch’s entire input, so its 67.42% on the six-class benchmark is a floor achieved with no learned representation. They are concatenated into the deployed hybrid classifier alongside the ConvNeXt embedding, so they contribute to the shipped prediction. And they are cheap and deterministic: the same crop yields the same 637 numbers on every machine, which the network’s embedding cannot promise across versions.

A · The histogram blocks

Colour, texture and edge: 627 of the 637 features are histogram bins or their descriptors.

Read the metal row against the organic row and the reason colour dominates becomes obvious.

Metal’s channels are three narrow separated spikes — a small number of surfaces, each near-uniform. Organic’s are broad overlapping humps spread across most of the range, because a piece of food waste is many colours at once. That difference in shape is what a tree ensemble can split on, and it is not available from a mean and a standard deviation, which is exactly why the full 256 bins are kept instead of six summary numbers.

Why both RGB and HSV? They fail differently. RGB entangles colour with brightness, so a shadowed white bottle and a grey one look alike. HSV separates hue from value, so hue survives a lighting change — and lighting change is the whole studio-versus-field problem. Keeping both means the classifier can use whichever is stable for a given crop.

Two honest findings here, and one of them is a flaw in the project’s own code.

Start with the LBP panel and note that bin 9 holds roughly 70% of the mass in every class. That bin is a catch-all: the implementation counts only the first ten uniform patterns and collapses all 198 non-uniform ones into bin 9, so most of the histogram lands there. It is documented in the extractor’s docstring rather than quietly fixed, because the deployed classifier was trained on features from this exact function — correcting the binning would shift the feature distribution and invalidate the shipped weights. Train/serve parity beat textbook LBP.

What about the GLCM panel? That one works as intended, and organic is the class it separates: lowest contrast, highest homogeneity and highest correlation of the six. Food waste is locally smooth in a way manufactured packaging is not, and the co-occurrence descriptors pick that up where the colour histogram cannot.

This is the largest single block — 324 of 637 features — so it is worth seeing what it encodes.

Look at the orientation histogram: the two tallest bars sit at 90° and 110°, with a third at 10°. That is the box’s edges, and it is a claim about geometry, not material. Which raises the obvious objection: why spend half the vector on shape when the whole project argues material is surface? Because HOG at a 16×16 cell on a 64×64 crop is not measuring the object outline. It is measuring local gradient structure within sixteen small tiles — corrugation, creases, print — which is texture described in the language of edges.

Does the evidence back that up? Yes — the domain-importance figures at the end of this chapter put edge/HOG at 36.5% of the tree ensemble’s total feature importance on the deployed vector (second only to colour) and at 59.6% on the analysis vector (first outright), while the explicit shape block (Hu moments, area, perimeter, circularity) carries 1.4%. If HOG were tracing outlines it would be measuring the same thing as that shape block, and would score like it. It does not.

B · Distributions across classes

Do the features actually separate the materials? Histograms over hundreds of real crops.

The answer is: barely, one at a time. That is the finding, not a disappointment.

Pick any panel and try to draw a line that separates two classes. There isn’t one. Every distribution overlaps every other heavily — the clearest signal in the whole figure is organic sitting slightly left on grad_mean and grad_std, which just says food waste has softer edges than packaging. This is precisely why the classical branch tops out in the sixties while the deep classifier reaches the low nineties: no single handcrafted descriptor is discriminative, so accuracy has to come from combining hundreds of weak ones.

Then why show a figure that undercuts the branch? Because the alternative is showing a feature table and letting the audience assume each row is informative. The tree ensembles do genuinely reach 67.42% on these features, and they do it by conditional combination — which is a much more interesting claim once you have seen how weak the individual axes are.

After the last figure’s overlap, here is a feature whose class ordering can be predicted before looking.

Read the right-hand ranking: paper 0.035, cardboard 0.031, then metal, plastic, glass, and organic last at 0.013. Fibrous printed surfaces carry the most fine detail; a smooth glass or plastic surface carries the least; food waste is the smoothest of all. That is the ordering material physics predicts, recovered from the data with no supervision. On the left, the same fact appears as paper and cardboard holding the highest curves in bands 6 to 8 while organic falls fastest.

Does one good feature rescue the classical branch? No — look at the error bars, which overlap between neighbouring classes. It is a real signal with a low ceiling on its own, and it earns its nine dimensions by being informative in combination, not alone.

Here the classical branch answers a question the deep model cannot: which kind of evidence is it actually using?

Read the four bars against the feature table at the top of this chapter. Colour and HOG together carry 85.7% of the decision, and shape — the Hu moments, area, perimeter and circularity — carries 1.4%. That is the project’s central claim about material recognition, measured rather than asserted: material identity is surface, not silhouette. Which is reassuring for a second reason too, given that crops arrive at arbitrary aspect ratios and get squashed to a square grid — a model leaning on shape would be leaning on a distortion artefact.

This is a testable claim, and Grad-CAM in the Experimental Results chapter independently confirms it on the deep side.

Why keep a classical branch at all? Because it produces explanations of a kind the deep model cannot. Feature importances are a property of the fitted trees, not a post-hoc reconstruction like a saliency map.

Two decompositions, one conclusion — which is the strongest form the claim can take.

Read this against Fig 5.6 and note what does not change. On the deployed vector, colour leads at 49.1% with edge/HOG second at 36.5%; on the analysis vector, HOG leads at 59.6% with colour second at 29.2%. The ordering of the top two swaps, because the two vectors spend their 637 dimensions differently — 324 HOG dimensions against 576. What survives both groupings is the part that matters: gradient structure and colour carry between 86% and 89% of the decision, and the explicitly geometric evidence carries almost none. Spatial statistics get 5.6% here; the shape block gets 1.4% there.

Why keep both figures rather than picking one? Because two different partitions of the same feature budget agreeing on the substance is a stronger result than either one alone. A finding that flipped when the grouping changed would be an artefact of the grouping.

06 / 14 Model Pipeline

Localize first, classify second.

Stage 1 finds objects, which is what YOLO is good at. Stage 2 verifies the material of each crop, which is what a dedicated classifier is good at, and filters false alarms with a Background veto class. The two chapters after this one train the two branches separately — classical machine learning first, then deep learning — so each is auditable on its own before they are compared.

S1
Upload image

A JPG, PNG, or WEBP photo of waste. Saved scans stay in browser history for review and hard-case collection.

S2
Stage 1: YOLO26m localization

YOLO26m proposes a box around every candidate object. 100-epoch retrain, promoted from YOLO26s for higher field recall and precision: 74.9% mAP50, 83.4% precision on clean validation.

S3
Crop extraction & size filter

Each kept box is cropped with 10 px padding; boxes smaller than 24×24 px are rejected before classification.

S4
Stage 2: crop verification

A ConvNeXt-Tiny + 637-handcrafted-feature classifier re-checks every crop across 7 classes, including a Background veto. Fine-tuned on the detector's own crops so training matches production.

S5
Score fusion, context prior & bin routing

Per-box score fuses detector and classifier confidences, a scene-context prior adjusts the class scores, and the dominant material is routed to its bin by a direct material→bin lookup.

i

The Background veto is the load-bearing wall. The Deep Learning chapter’s detector confusion matrix shows the detector’s errors are misses and false alarms, not material mix-ups — a second stage that re-judges every crop, plus a Background class that can veto, is aimed at exactly that error profile.

i

How every model after this point is scored. Fixing the measuring stick before any model appears is what makes the next four chapters comparable. Localisation is matched to ground truth by intersection over union at 0.50, greedily and one-to-one: a matched pair is a true positive, an unmatched prediction a false positive, an unmatched ground-truth box a false negative, and precision, recall and F1 follow from those three counts. Average precision is the area under a class’s precision-recall curve, mAP50 averages it over the six materials, and mAP50-95 averages again over ten IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95, which penalises loose boxes that mAP50 alone accepts. Classification uses accuracy and macro-F1, the unweighted mean across classes, so a small class cannot be carried by a large one. Every model is scored twice wherever leakage was possible — once on the original split, once on the quarantined clean split — and only the clean number is claimed. One limitation bounds all of it: each model was trained once, with no seed sweep, so every figure is a single-run point estimate. Gaps under roughly one percentage point are reported as ties and never used to support a claim.

Five steps, one flow. Follow the data from S1 to S5 and watch where it narrows.

Look at the size filter between S3 and S4. It is the only step that discards data, and it is there for a reason. Boxes under 24×24 px are rejected before classification, because a crop that small carries almost no material texture — and the classifier will still return a confident label for it. Refusing to classify beats a confident guess the interface presents as a result.

Does that filter throw away real detections? Yes, and it is a genuine trade. It is also why small-object detection recall is tracked separately, so the cost of this filter stays visible instead of being absorbed into a single accuracy figure.

Split that flow in two. First, the deep path — the one that actually ships.

Notice the concatenation node where the ConvNeXt embedding meets the 637 handcrafted features. This is a hybrid, not a pure deep model. The learned embedding and the hand-designed colour, FFT, HOG and spatial features feed one head together. The handcrafted half is cheap, deterministic, and encodes exactly the evidence the classical branch showed matters most for material.

Do the handcrafted features earn their place? They are the same 637 features the classical branch reaches 67.42% with on their own, so they carry real signal rather than noise. Keeping the two branches feature-compatible is what makes the classical model an interpretable stand-in for the deep one.

And the classical path, running on identical inputs.

Notice that it starts from the same crops as the deep branch. That is what makes the comparison fair. This branch is not a fallback, and it is not padding. It is a controlled comparison: same crops, same splits, same metrics, different hypothesis class. Without it, “the deep model is better” would be an assumption rather than a measurement.

Did it earn its place? It is the branch that discovered the domain-shift problem. Training it on studio images and testing on field images exposed the 39.0% collapse — the finding that reframed the rest of the project around field data.

The same pipeline a third time — now ordered by time rather than by data.

Look at the single request–response pair between browser and API. One round trip, not one per object. Both stages run server-side inside a single request, because sending crops back to the browser for a second call would multiply latency by the number of detections — and a cluttered scene can produce a dozen. The sequence view is how you see that cost before you build it.

What about latency? It is reported live on the demo page after every scan, so the claim is checkable rather than asserted.

And this is where it actually runs.

Notice the split: a static frontend on one host, a model server carrying the checkpoints on another. The two halves have completely different requirements. The page is static files and can be cached at the edge; the models need a Python runtime and hundreds of megabytes of weights. Keeping them apart means this page stays up even when the model host is asleep.

What happens when the backend is down? The status indicator in the header turns over and the page states it plainly. It does not fall back to a canned result and present it as a live scan — the easy demo-day shortcut, and a dishonest one.

07 / 14 Machine Learning Models

The ML track: tree ensembles on 637 handcrafted features.

The classical branch is kept as a separate track so it can be tracked on its own terms. It sees exactly the crops the deep branch sees, summarized into the feature vector of the previous chapter, and is fitted with six model families. The best of them, XGBoost, reaches 67.42% accuracy and 0.6506 macro-F1 on the balanced six-class benchmark — roughly 26 points behind the deployed deep classifier, fully inspectable, and the branch that discovered the project’s domain-shift problem.

i

Why the ML track is not a loss-curve chapter. These models are fitted on an already-extracted feature matrix, not trained end-to-end on pixels by gradient descent, so there is no per-epoch loss to plot. The right artifacts are confusion matrices, ranked accuracy and macro-F1, and dimensionality sweeps — all four of which are below.

Start with the whole classical family, ranked, so the choice made inside it is defensible before anything is compared to a network.

Notice the tree ensembles clustered at the top and the linear models trailing. The split is not arbitrary. Linear models need the classes to be separable by a hyperplane in the raw 637-dimensional space, and material identity is not linear in colour histograms and HOG bins. Tree ensembles carve the space with axis-aligned thresholds and can combine features conditionally — which is what this problem needs.

Why plot both accuracy and macro-F1? To show they agree. Where they agree, the balanced benchmark is doing its job and no class is being quietly ignored.

The winner of that ranking, class by class — and the honest shape of a 67% model.

Notice how much mass sits off the diagonal. Against the deep classifiers in the Model Comparison chapter the gap is roughly 26 points, and it is not close. Stated plainly: accuracy is not what the classical branch is for. What it offers instead is that every decision traces to a named feature and a threshold you can read.

Is the classical branch just decoration, then? No, on two counts. It supplies the 637 features the deployed hybrid actually consumes, and it is the branch that found the domain-shift result at the end of this chapter.

Read this one across, not down. The columns are the story, and they are more dramatic than any row.

Trace the XGBoost row: 65.0%, 81.0%, 87.6%, 14.0%. One model, four datasets, a 73-point spread. Within any column, the best model beats the worst by about 20 points. Moving between columns swings accuracy by up to 73. The dataset — its class count and its domain — dominates the model choice completely. Any paper reporting a single accuracy without naming the dataset is reporting almost nothing.

Why does the TACO column collapse to single digits? 39 classes with very low support each, and real-world occlusion. It is included precisely because it is unflattering; leaving it out would make the classical branch look far stronger than it is.

A fair question at this point: are 637 features actually needed, or is that just padding?

Look at the knee of the explained-variance curve, where a few dozen components already account for most variance. Read naively, this curve says the feature vector could be compressed by an order of magnitude almost for free. That reading is wrong, and the next figure is the reason. Explained variance measures how much the data spreads along a component — not how much that component helps separate the classes.

Why test it at all? Because 637 features per crop is real inference cost, and cutting it would have been worth having. It was tested rather than assumed — and the answer came back no.

And the answer is no: the tree ensembles need almost the whole vector to reach their ceiling.

Notice where each curve flattens — close to the full dimensionality, not at the variance knee. This is the pair of figures that makes the point. Variance concentrates in a few dozen components; accuracy does not. Low-variance components still carry class-discriminative information, so PCA chosen by explained variance would have quietly cost accuracy. Two plots, and the intuitive answer turns out to be the wrong one.

The cost of compressing is model-specific, which is why no single “PCA costs about 2%” claim is made. Squeezed from 637 to 128 components — still 99.90% of the variance — Linear SVM drops 62.43% → 59.90%, a loss of 2.52 points, while Logistic Regression drops only 60.33% → 59.71%, a loss of 0.62.

What does this mean for deployment? The 637-feature extraction stays in the pipeline at full width. The compression opportunity was tested and declined on evidence.

The result that changed the project

Train the classical model on one acquisition domain, test it on the other.

!

The gotcha the classical branch exposed. Train the 637-feature model on studio photos and test it on real-world field photos, and accuracy falls off a cliff: from 79.8% in-domain down to 39.0%. Studio and field photos are the same materials lit and framed differently, and that gap is the whole reason field-domain data and matched operating points matter more than another point of benchmark accuracy.

If one figure from this project is worth remembering, it is this one. Give it time.

Start with the diagonal: both in-domain numbers are respectable. Then move to the off-diagonal and let the drop land. Nothing changed except which photographs the model had seen. Same seven materials, same features, same training procedure. Cross the domain boundary and accuracy roughly halves, to 39.0% — which on seven classes is not far above chance for the harder classes. A benchmark number tells you almost nothing about a domain the model has not met.

Why is the gap asymmetric — 40.7 points one way and 28.3 the other? Because field data is more varied, so a model trained on it transfers to clean studio images better than the reverse. Diversity in training buys generalisation. That asymmetry is the direct argument for the hard-negative mining on new field data in the Experimental Results chapter, and it is why the deployed pipeline trains on both.

08 / 14 Deep Learning Models

The DL track: one detector, then the classifier shoot-out.

The deep track splits cleanly in two, and is presented that way. Stage 1 is the detector: a 100-epoch YOLO26m retrain, promoted from YOLO26s after it won on field recall. Stage 2 is the classifier: the deployed ConvNeXt-Tiny + 637-feature hybrid, fine-tuned on the detector’s own crops so training matches production, plus the four comparison models (EfficientNetB0, ResNet50, MobileNetV2, a from-scratch tiny CNN) trained on the same balanced crop benchmark. ResNet50 and MobileNetV2 were retrained for 15 epochs so their curves show where each model stops learning and starts memorizing; the earlier 2-epoch runs were too short to read.

A · Stage 1 — the detector

Training curves and the error profile that justifies a second stage.

Start with the model that is actually deployed, then work outwards to what it beat.

Look at the train and validation loss curves: they stay together for the full 100 epochs. That absence of divergence is the interesting result. Train and validation track each other to the end, so the run stopped for budget reasons, not because the model started memorising. Capacity is not the binding constraint here — consistent with everything the Discussion chapter concludes about data being the lever.

Was it under-trained? Possibly, and that is the honest answer. The curves flatten but never turn, so more epochs would likely buy a little more. It was not pursued because the field-recall gap was an order of magnitude larger than anything another 50 epochs would close.

Here is what it replaced — and why the replacement decision was harder than it looks.

Notice the plateau. The plateau is not what settled the choice. Comparing two detectors by their curves is a trap, because each curve is drawn at its own default confidence threshold; two models can swap places entirely once you hold the operating point fixed. The promotion was decided by re-running both at the same threshold on the same field set, where YOLO26m won on recall — 78.1% against 70.9%.

Why did clean-validation recall dip slightly on the newer model? Because it did, from 69.0% to 67.2%, and that is reported rather than hidden. Studio-validation recall is not the metric this system is optimised for; field recall is, and it rose by 7.2 points.

This matrix is the single best justification for the whole two-stage design.

Look at the background row and column, then at how clean the off-diagonal material cells are by comparison. The detector almost never calls glass “metal”. What it does is miss objects and invent them. Those are localization errors, not material errors. A deeper detector would not fix that — but a second stage that only judges already-cropped objects, plus a Background class that can veto false alarms, addresses exactly this error profile.

Why normalized rather than raw counts? Because the classes have different support: raw counts would make the largest class look like the worst offender purely by volume. Row-normalising makes each row a per-class error rate.

B · Stage 2 — the deployed classifier

The model actually served, and the domain split its pooled accuracy was hiding.

i

Read the curve, not just the final number. ResNet50’s validation loss bottoms out at epoch 3 of 15; everything after that is memorization wearing the costume of more training. MobileNetV2 buys three extra epochs before the same thing happens, at a tenth of the parameters, which is why parameter count alone never settles a model choice here.

Every other classifier confusion matrix in this chapter is a model that was rejected. This is the one that ships.

Start with the Background column — specifically the Plastic row's 6% leaking into it, the biggest off-diagonal cell in the grid. The failure that matters is not a material mix-up; it is the veto firing on a real object. Twenty-five plastic crops were called Background, which in production means the item silently does not appear in the results. That is a miss the user cannot see, which makes it worse than a visible wrong label.

Why is Cardboard the strongest class at 98%? It has the most distinctive texture of the seven — corrugated edges and a consistent matte brown — and it is well represented in both studio and field sources. The classes that suffer are the ones that depend on specular highlights, which lighting destroys.

This is the figure that answers the worry raised back at the WaDaBa sample grid in the Dataset chapter.

Read the two accuracy figures in the panel titles first — 96.5% against 91.3% — then notice how much emptier the studio matrix is. A single pooled number, 92.8%, would have concealed a five-point domain gap. The studio panel is nearly diagonal, with three classes at 100%, because those images are trivially separable. The field panel is where the real errors live, and field is what the deployed system meets.

Does this contradict the 39.0% cross-domain collapse in the Machine Learning chapter? No, and the distinction is important. There, a model is trained on one domain and tested on a domain it has never seen. Here, both domains are in training, and the residual five-point gap is the honest difficulty difference that remains once the model has seen both. That difference is the entire argument for training on field data.

Its confusion matrix — and the one error that survives into every model in this project.

Notice the paper and cardboard cells trading mass with each other. This is the only structured error in the matrix, and it is not really a model failure. Cardboard is paper, mechanically pressed. A cropped fragment of a flattened box under poor lighting is genuinely ambiguous, and human annotators disagree on the same crops.

Does it matter operationally? Much less than the matrix suggests, because paper and cardboard route to the same bin. The routing table absorbs this particular confusion — worth stating plainly so it is not mistaken for a hidden failure.

This is the curve the callout above is talking about. Trace it left to right.

Find epoch 3 on the validation loss curve — the lowest point — then follow the line as it drifts back up while train loss keeps falling. That divergence is textbook overfitting, and it starts twelve epochs before training ends. Everything after epoch 3 makes the model fit the training set better and the world slightly worse. The final accuracy number, 92.2%, hides all of this: the curve is the evidence, the number is just a summary.

Why not stop at epoch 3, then? For a deployed model you would, with early stopping. This run was deliberately allowed to finish because its purpose was to show where each architecture breaks — which a stopped run cannot.

Its smaller sibling, same schedule — and the comparison makes a general point about capacity.

Look at epoch 6, where this one bottoms out, and contrast it with epoch 3 on the previous figure. Capacity is a two-sided knob here: less of it delays memorisation and caps the ceiling at the same time. That is why parameter count alone never settles a model choice.

Why does it lose to ResNet50 specifically? The classes it gives up ground on are glass against plastic, which separate on fine specular texture. MobileNetV2's thin depthwise feature layers under-represent exactly that.

All three classifiers, same crops, same split, side by side. Read it left to right.

Watch the diagonal sharpen across the three panels, then find the paper–cardboard cell appearing in all three. Two things are visible at once: the ranking — 91.4%, then 92.2%, then 94.3% — and the fact that all three fail on the same pair. A shared error across three different architectures is not an architecture problem; it is telling you something about the data. That is the difference between reading a confusion matrix and reading an accuracy table.

Why are the panels comparable? 300 crops per class in every panel, one balanced test split, one loader. The only variable is the model.

And last, the floor: what a deep model is worth with no transfer learning at all.

Look at the final accuracy, 48.1%, then back up at the 94.3% on the comparison grid. This is the control experiment for the whole deep branch. Same architecture family, same data, same schedule; the only thing removed is the pretrained weights. Nearly half the accuracy goes with them. So the honest claim is not “deep learning solves this” — it is “ImageNet-scale pretraining solves this, and a 31k-image dataset is nowhere near enough to learn material texture from scratch”.

Would a bigger scratch model close the gap? Unlikely at this data scale, and the classical branch is the evidence. Tree ensembles on 637 hand-designed features reach 67.42%, well above any from-scratch network here, because they encode prior knowledge the network would have to learn from data it does not have.

The four metric cards above are single points. This is the whole surface they were chosen from.

Pick one curve and slide along it. 83.4% precision and 67.2% recall is one point on that curve — any other point could have been picked instead. Quoting precision and recall as if they were fixed properties of a detector is the most common mistake in this kind of report. They are a choice. Anyone can raise either one by moving the threshold; what cannot be moved is the curve itself. That is why mAP, the area underneath it, is the threshold-free comparison.

Why do the classes separate so widely? Because per-class AP tracks object size and source almost exactly. The classes sitting lowest are the ones Fig 3.3 flagged as majority-small-box.

And here is why the deployed threshold deliberately sits off the peak.

Find the peak of the F1 curve, then look slightly to its left — that is where the system actually runs. Peak F1 is only the right answer if a false positive and a false negative cost the same. For this application they do not. A missed bottle is waste that never gets sorted; a false alarm is a box the user glances at and dismisses. So the threshold is lowered on purpose, buying recall with precision.

Is that post-hoc justification? No — it is measured. The sweep in Fig 10.3 shows the exact recall gained at each threshold, in both domains, and the choice was made from that table.

The expected result here was a trade-off. There isn't one, and that is worth pausing on.

Read the field small-box row left to right: 31.8, 35.6, 39.0, 43.2. Then watch the studio row do the same thing. The assumption going in was that tuning the detector towards field conditions would cost studio performance. It does not. Both domains gain, and the small-box rows gain fastest, because small objects are exactly the detections that sit just under a high confidence bar. This is the cheapest improvement in the entire project: no retraining, one config value.

What does it cost? Precision, which is not on this chart and deserves to be stated rather than skipped. More boxes are proposed, so more of them are wrong. For this application that is the right side to err on, and the Background veto in stage two removes a large share of the extra false alarms before the user sees them.

Read this as a floor, not an average. The question it answers is “is any class quietly broken?”

Start with the two lightest cells: Metal precision 0.884 and Paper recall 0.882. Those are the worst numbers on the whole grid. A headline accuracy can hide a class sitting at 40% if the others carry it. Here the weakest single cell is 0.882, so nothing is being carried. The asymmetry is readable too: Metal's precision is lower than its recall, meaning the model over-assigns Metal, while Paper is the reverse — under-assigned, because some of it is being called Cardboard.

Why does this differ from the 3,194-crop matrix in the Deep Learning chapter? Different evaluation set. This is the clean hard-case split from convnext_clean_eval.json; Fig 8.4 is the merged clean test split. Both are leakage-quarantined, both use the same weights, and each figure states its own n so the two are never confused.

11 / 14 Experimental Results

What the experiments actually changed.

The headline experiments: fine-tuning the classifier on the detector's own crops lifted production-distribution accuracy from 76.91% to 88.88% while clean-crop accuracy also improved (92.93% → 93.77%). Promoting YOLO26s to a 100-epoch YOLO26m retrain lifted field recall from 70.9% to 78.1% and field small-object recall from 58.6% to 68.2% at the same threshold. Hard-negative mining on new field data (PlastOPol) lifted held-out small-object recall from 38.6% to 54.7%. New data, not architecture, was the lever.

✓

Every lift in this section came from data, not architecture: crop fine-tuning +12.0 points, hard-negative mining +16.1 points, the YOLO26m promotion +7.2 points of field recall. That pattern — data is the lever — is the project’s main lesson, and the new training round in the Model Comparison chapter confirms it from the architecture side.

Enough charts. This is the pipeline doing its job on the conditions it was built for.

Follow the output in order: the box, then the material label, then the bin route. That order is the pipeline. This is a single object on a cluttered natural background — the easy end of the field domain, and still much harder than any studio image in Fig 2.5. Grass is high-frequency texture that a detector can easily propose boxes on; the Background class is what stops that.

Were these examples cherry-picked? These three were chosen to span easy, small-object, and cluttered cases rather than to show only successes. The live demo in the Live Demo takes arbitrary uploads, which is the real test.

Next, the exact regime the EDA warned about: several small objects in one frame.

Find the smallest can in the frame — and note honestly any object the detector missed. This is where localize-first pays off most visibly. A whole-image classifier returns one label for this photo. The two-stage pipeline returns one verified material per can, each with its own confidence, because each crop is judged on its own.

What about the misses? Field small-object recall is 68.2% at the deployed operating point, so roughly one small object in three is still missed. That is the project's largest open weakness, and the Discussion chapter states it as such.

And the hardest case: clutter, overlap, mixed materials, and indoor lighting all at once.

Look at the regions with no box on them — a counter edge, a reflective surface — that a naive detector would have fired on. The Background class is doing the visible work here. In a scene this busy the detector proposes far more regions than there are objects, and without a veto stage the interface would fill with confident labels on worktops and shadows. Suppressing a proposal is a result, even though it leaves nothing on screen to see.

Why are overlapping objects still hard? Crops of overlapping items contain two materials, and the classifier is trained to return one. That is a known limitation of crop-level verification, not a bug in this particular scene.

Now a different question entirely. Not “is it right?” but “is it right for the right reason?”

Notice the hot region — and, just as importantly, the cool background around it. This directly answers the worry raised back at Fig 2.5. If the classifier had learned studio backgrounds instead of materials, the heat would sit on the surroundings. It sits on the object surface. This is the deep branch independently agreeing with the classical branch's feature importances in Fig 5.6 and Fig 5.7, which found colour and gradient structure dominant and explicit geometry negligible on both decompositions of the feature vector.

How much weight should Grad-CAM carry? It is supporting evidence, not proof. Saliency maps are known to be reconstructions rather than the model's actual reasoning. It is convincing here because it agrees with a second, independent line of evidence.

The same check, now on a field-domain item — because the studio case alone would prove nothing.

Notice where the activation concentrates: the specular highlights on the surface and the label region. Plastic and glass are the pair most at risk of being separated by background cues rather than material, since studio glass and studio plastic sit on the same white sweep. Here, on a field photo, the model is keying on highlight structure and label texture — properties of the object itself.

Does that generalise to every field image? No, and the domain confusion matrix in the Deep Learning chapter gives the honest aggregate: 91.3% on field crops against 96.5% on studio. The gap is real; this figure explains what the model uses when it gets it right.

Not the detector and not the classifier — the assembled system, including the rules that turn scores into a bin.

238.08 ms per image on CPU across a 100-image sweep of the complete deployed path: Stage 1, the adaptive engine, Stage 2, score fusion and routing. The sweep produced 348 YOLO proposals, of which 295 survived Stage 2 verification.

On an independent 1,042-crop set. Macro-recall over the five classes actually present is 84.60%, about ten points lower, and that is the more honest single figure — paper and plastic are over 80% of this set.

Reported for completeness only. Five of the six materials route to one Recycling bin, so 99.6% of this set is truly Recycling and a constant “Recycling” predictor beats the pipeline. It measures how rarely the system declines to commit, not routing competence.

A rule you keep because it feels prudent is still an untested rule. This is what happened when it was tested.

Compare the second group against the first. Removing the waste-state gate outright is the best configuration on every aggregate metric — bin routing 97.41% against 96.26%, waste-state 97.98% against 96.74% — which is the measured basis for deleting the gate rather than tuning it. A conservative rule that fires on uncertainty was costing accuracy, not buying safety.

Now look at how little the bars differ overall. Across all eight retained gated variants the spread in material accuracy is under one percentage point. That is the honest reading of this whole subsection: the decision layer was never the binding constraint on the system’s accuracy. Localisation recall was, and still is.

Then why show four near-identical bar groups? Because a negative result is the point. Without this figure, deleting a production rule looks like a hunch. With it, the deletion is the configuration that won on evidence.

12 / 14 Discussion

Measured limits, and what was done about them.

Domain shift Partly fixed, partly open

Fixed: the classifier was fine-tuned on the detector's own crops, lifting production-distribution accuracy from 76.9% to 88.9%. The detector was re-tuned for the field domain too: lowering the confidence threshold and hard-negative mining on new field data (PlastOPol) lifted held-out field small-object recall from 38.6% to 54.7%, at a small cost to studio mAP50 (72.1% → 70.9%). Open: on the fully unseen clean test split the detector still drops to 0.482 mAP50 against 0.749 on clean validation; the gap is narrowing with new data, not fully closed.

Tiny objects Resolution ruled out; new data confirmed as the lever

A 960px training run was evaluated: validation unchanged, unseen-batch recall up 3.9 points, not enough to chase. A later hard-negative mining fine-tune on new field data confirmed the real lever: small-object recall on that held-out domain rose 38.6% to 54.7% from new data alone, no resolution or architecture change. Small objects remain the main source of misses; training-data diversity is the binding constraint.

Material-first routing No disposability judgment

The app reports what an object is made of and routes that material to its bin. It does not judge whether the item should actually be thrown away; that waste-state decision was removed because there was no dataset to train it reliably.

Method note. Metrics on this site come from the leakage-audited evaluation sets. The audit scripts, quarantine manifest, and reports live in the project repository. The Stage-2 architecture shoot-out (EfficientNetB0, ResNet50, MobileNetV2) is measured on the balanced crop benchmark that predates the quarantine, so all three share one test set and stay comparable to each other, but not to the 93.77% clean-test headline.
